## TIMELINE ENTRIES

### Entry 1 — ca. 1100–1150: Hashish Enters Egypt via Sufi Mystics
Cannabis as an intoxicant was used in Persia and Iraq by the late 9th century but its spread to Egypt came with Sufi mystics arriving from Syria during the Ayyubid period. Medieval Arabic sources, compiled by Rosenthal, indicate that itinerant dervishes introduced the plant as a vehicle for *dhikr* (the remembrance of God), using it to induce states of ecstasy and mystical union (*fana*). The word *hashish* appears in Arabic texts by 1123.

**Source:** Rosenthal, *The Herb* (1971); Nahas (PMC1805385); medievalists.net/2012/06/hashish-in-islam-9th-to-18th-century/

---

### Entry 2 — 1253: Cairo's First Great Crackdown
Egypt's Ayyubid/early Mamluk authorities issued the first recorded edicts against cannabis cultivation and hashish sale in 1253. Sufis had been openly growing cannabis in Cairo gardens; the government, framing Sufi practices as a threat to social order, raided their farms and destroyed the crops. This is the earliest documented state suppression of cannabis in the Arabic-speaking world.

**Source:** MIT Press Reader (thereader.mitpress.mit.edu); Seedsman Blog; Cannabis in Egypt (Wikipedia snippets)

**Extract:** Sufis were "openly growing marijuana in Cairo" and the government "raided their farms and destroyed all their crops." — *Seedsman Blog, summarizing Arabic chronicle sources*

---

### Entry 3 — 1266 & 1378: Mamluk Crackdowns and the Tooth-Puller's Decree
Mamluk Sultan Baybars I (r. 1260–1277) ordered death by the sword for hashish consumers in 1266/7, though this was rarely enforced. The most notorious crackdown came in 1378, when Mamluk emir **Soudoun ash-Shaykhūnī** (Soudoun Sheikhouni) decreed that users caught with hashish would have their molar teeth extracted. Cannabis fields and entire farming villages were burned; many suffered dental mutilation, imprisonment, or execution. Despite this martial response, demand persisted.

**Source:** Seedsman Blog; Medievalists.net search snippets; Cannanaskis.com

**Extract:** "Soudoun Sheikhouni decreed that the molar teeth of people making hashish should be extracted; many suffered this fate." — *Multiple sources summarizing Mamluk chronicles*

**Fun Fact:** The 1378 decree is considered one of the earliest cannabis prohibition laws in world history — and it was almost immediately flouted.

---

### Entry 4 — ca. 13th–14th Century: Ibn Taymiyya and the Jurisprudence Wars
The Hanbali scholar Ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328) became the most influential Islamic voice against hashish, arguing it was *worse* than wine. He recommended 80 lashes for users and held that hashish corrupted both body and soul. On the other side, Hanafi judge Jamal al-Din al-Malati (d. 1400) issued a rare *fatwa* permitting hashish use. This jurisprudential split — haram versus tolerated — ran through centuries of Islamic legal discourse, with scholars debating whether hashish constituted *khamr* (wine-like intoxicant) under Quranic prohibition.

**Source:** abukhadeejah.com; islamweb.net; islamqa.info; Rosenthal *The Herb*

**Extract (Ibn Taymiyya):** *"All the faults, blemishes, and other bad things in khamr (wine) are present in hashish, and more, because the majority of faults in khamr affect religion, but hashish affects, to a great extent, both religion and body."* — Ibn Taymiyya, 14th century fatwa, as cited in Islamic jurisprudence sources

---

### Entry 5 — October 1800: Napoleon's Ban — Europe's First Modern Drug Prohibition
When French forces invaded Egypt in 1798, alcohol was unavailable (Islamic country) so troops resorted to hashish, which they found to their liking. In October 1800, General **Jacques-François Menou** — left in command after Napoleon's departure — issued a formal proclamation banning the manufacture and sale of hashish and ordering the closure of all hashish cafes. The decree stated that hashish caused users to "lose reason and fall into a violent delirium, which often leads them to commit excesses of all kinds." The ban is widely regarded as one of the first examples of modern Western drug prohibition legislation — and it was almost entirely ineffective.

**Source:** Organic CBD LLC; Herbal Dispatch; Napoleon, Cannabis and Hemp (cannahealthamsterdam.com); The Real History of France's First Anti-Drug Law (pointshistory.org)

**Extract (Menou Decree):** Hashish "cause[s] its users to lose reason and fall into a violent delirium, which often leads them to commit excesses of all kinds." — General Menou, Proclamation, October 1800

### Entry 6 — 1925: Egypt Leads the Global War on Hashish
At the 1925 Geneva International Convention on Narcotics Control, Egypt (backed by Turkey and supported by Italy and South Africa) successfully lobbied to include cannabis alongside opium and cocaine in mandatory international drug controls — becoming the first such global restriction on cannabis. Egypt's stated justification was that "chronic hashism" was causing widespread insanity, though critics noted that similar consumption in India produced no such crisis. Some historians have suggested Egypt was also protecting its cotton-export industry from hemp competition.

**Source:** ResearchGate (Cannabis Prohibition in Egypt, 1880–1939); TNI (tni.org); Cambridge Core (American Journal of International Law); Cairo Scene

### Entry 7 — Morocco and the Rif: Kif Culture and the World's Hash Bowl
Cannabis arrived in Morocco between the 7th and 15th centuries via Arab merchants and Andalusian refugees. In the northern **Rif Mountains**, particularly around **Ketama** (Al-Hoceima province), the plant adapted to mountain terrain and became woven into Berber village life. Dried cannabis mixed with tobacco, smoked in the elegant long-stemmed **sebsi** pipe, was called *kif* and was a cornerstone of social hospitality. Large-scale **hashish** production — using the sieving technique learned from Lebanon — began in the 1960s as European demand surged. By the early 2000s, Morocco supplied roughly 31% of the world's hashish.

**Source:** PMC (ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/PMC9587784); Alchimia Grow Shop; Cannabis in Morocco (Wikipedia snippets); GnomesGarden blog; High Times

**Fun Fact:** Moroccan hashish techniques were not traditional — the sieve/press method arrived from Lebanon during the 1960s hippie trail.

---

### Entry 8 — Lebanon's Bekaa Valley: The Red Leb Legacy
Lebanon's **Bekaa Valley** has been a cannabis-growing region for centuries, its warm, continental climate and fertile alluvial soil producing resin-rich plants. Lebanese "Red Leb" and "Blonde Leb" became globally prized varieties in the 1970s. During the Lebanese Civil War (1975–1990), Bekaa cannabis became a major revenue source for militias and warlords. The French Mandate period (1920–1943) had allowed cultivation; subsequent Lebanese governments attempted prohibition, though enforcement was minimal in militia-controlled zones.

**Source:** Alchimia Grow Shop (cannabis-lebanon article); Hurcann blog; ScienceDirect (Hashish in Morocco and Lebanon comparative study)

---

### Entry 9 — Ethiopia: Pipes, Lalibela, and the Coptic Tradition
Archaeological ceramic smoking pipes found in **Lalibela Cave**, Begemeder province, Ethiopia, dated to approximately **1320 CE**, contained traces of cannabis-derived substances — among the earliest physical evidence of cannabis consumption in sub-Saharan Africa. Ethiopia has long cultivated cannabis, and the **Coptic Church of Alexandria** is believed to have incorporated cannabis in certain ritual contexts. The **Ethiopian Zion Coptic Church** (formally incorporated in Jamaica/Florida in the 20th century) claims an ancient Ethiopian precedent for sacramental cannabis use — though this continuity is debated by historians.

**Source:** Sensi Seeds (cannabis-in-ethiopia article); druglibrary.net; en-academic.com (Ethiopian Zion Coptic Church)

---

### Entry 10 — Greece: Tekedes, Rebetiko, and the Hash Dens of Piraeus
Greek port cities — especially **Piraeus**, Athens, and Thessaloniki — developed a distinctive hashish-den culture called the **tekedes** (singular: *tekes*), where hashish was smoked with hookahs and water pipes to the sound of improvised **rebetiko** music. The rebetiko genre — sometimes called "the Greek blues" — emerged from the margins: sailors, refugees from the 1922 Asia Minor catastrophe, dock workers, and the urban poor. The **hasiklidika** (hashish songs) were a central sub-genre. **Markos Vamvakaris**, patriarch of rebetiko, performed in Piraeus tekedes as part of the legendary "Piraeus Quartet" (with Batis, Delias, and Pagioumtzis). Hashish traffic between southern Greece and North Africa's megacities (Cairo, Alexandria) was extensive. In **1936**, the Metaxas dictatorship closed all tekedes, banned hundreds of songs, and forced artists like Vamvakaris to modify lyrics. Female stars **Rita Abatzi** and **Rosa Eszkenazi** also navigated censorship in this era.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Rebetiko search snippets); Greek City Times; FolkWorld #44; Metaxas Project; greeksongstories.wordpress.com

## FUN FACTS

1. **The Hashshashin Myth:** The feared medieval Nizari Ismaili assassins were called "Hashshashin" by their enemies as a pejorative (meaning roughly "rabble" or "disreputable people"), not because they used hashish. The hashish-fueled-killers legend was popularized by Marco Polo's secondhand account (written after the fortress of Alamut had already been destroyed) and given academic authority by French orientalist Silvestre de Sacy in 1809 — a narrative now widely debunked by Islamic historians.

2. **Haydar's Walk:** A beloved Sufi legend holds that the Persian mystic **Qutb al-Din Haydar** (d. 1221), founder of a major dervish order, discovered a cannabis plant during a solitary walk — the only plant moving in still air — ate some leaves, and returned joyful and vivified. He reportedly told his disciples about the plant's properties but swore them to Sufi secrecy.

3. **Napoleon's Troops Got Too High:** When French soldiers in Egypt discovered hashish in 1798, many indulged enthusiastically — prompting their commanders to issue a full ban two years later. The troops' experience likely helped introduce hashish to European consciousness, ultimately inspiring the Club des Hashischins in 1840s Paris.

4. **Teeth as Punishment:** Mamluk emir Soudoun Sheikhouni's 1378 decree didn't call for flogging or imprisonment as the primary punishment — it mandated that hashish users have their molars pulled. Dentistry as drug enforcement may be unique in world legal history.

5. **Egypt vs. The World, 1925:** Egypt's aggressive lobbying at the League of Nations in 1925 resulted in cannabis being placed under the same international controls as heroin and cocaine — making Egypt the unlikely architect of global cannabis prohibition, even while its own population remained enthusiastic consumers.

6. **Kif Is Not Hashish:** In Morocco, *kif* traditionally refers to dried cannabis flowers mixed with tobacco and smoked in a sebsi pipe — not pressed hashish resin. The hashish sieve method only arrived from Lebanon in the 1960s, meaning Morocco's centurues-old cannabis culture was based on flower smoking, not the hash blocks it's now famous for.

7. **Rebetiko UNESCO Recognition:** Rebetiko music — born in Greek hashish dens — was inscribed on the **UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage** list in 2017, giving official global recognition to a musical genre rooted in the tekes and the smoke of hashish.

8. **Ibn al-Baytar's Egyptian Field Notes:** The 13th-century botanist Ibn al-Baytar, traveling through Egypt around 1230, observed locals eating cannabis leaves — one of the first documented descriptions of hashish consumption in Egypt by a named observer. His botanical encyclopedia (*Kitab al-Jami fi al-Adwiya al-Mufrada*) remains a primary source cited by Rosenthal.
