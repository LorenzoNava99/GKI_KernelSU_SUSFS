# Archaeology & the Latest Science: Cannabis & Hashish Through Deep Time
*Facet: Genomics · Residue Analysis · Archaeobotany · 2018–2026 Frontiers*

---

## Overview

Modern archaeology has transformed our understanding of cannabis from a folk curiosity into one of humanity's oldest cultivated plants. Since 2018, a convergence of whole-genome sequencing, gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) residue analysis, phytolith studies, and morphometric seed analysis has produced an unprecedented picture: cannabis was domesticated in East Asia at least 12,000 years ago, ritually smoked by Pamir plateau communities 2,500 years ago, burned on Israelite altars in 700 BCE, and woven into burial shrouds across the Xinjiang oasis towns of the Silk Road. Each discovery is a node in a much older, richer story than previously imagined.

## Key Findings — Annotated Research Entries

### 1. Cannabis Domesticated ~12,000 Years Ago in East Asia
**Citation:** Ren G., Zhang X., Li Y. et al. "Large-scale whole-genome resequencing unravels the domestication history of *Cannabis sativa*." *Science Advances* 7(29): eabg2286 (16 July 2021). DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.abg2286

**Finding:** Whole-genome resequencing of 110 *Cannabis sativa* accessions from worldwide origins — including feral populations, landraces, hemp cultivars, and drug-type varieties — revealed that the plant was first domesticated approximately **12,000 years ago in early Neolithic East Asia**. All modern hemp and drug cultivars diverge from an ancestral gene pool still represented by feral plants and landraces in China. A decisive fiber/drug split occurred ~4,000 years ago, driven by selective breeding that targeted cellulose/lignin biosynthesis genes (for fiber) versus THCA/CBDA synthase genes (for cannabinoid potency).

**Location:** East Asia (origin); 110 globally distributed accessions analyzed.
**Method:** Whole-genome resequencing; 12 million single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers; phylogenomic clustering; selection-sweep analysis.
**Significance:** Settles the long-debated "single domestication" question — one origin, in China, from which all cultivated cannabis descended.

**Fun fact:** Hemp-type and drug-type cannabis are each other's closest relatives — more alike genomically than either is to the wild feral populations still found in China's river valleys.

**Artifact/Illustration note:** Visualize a phylogenetic tree radiating from a single node in East Asia, with fiber-type and drug-type branches diverging 4,000 years ago like two rivers from one mountain spring.

### 2. Ritual Cannabis Smoking in the Pamir Mountains, 500 BCE
**Citation:** Ren M., Tang Z., Wu X. et al. "The origins of cannabis smoking: Chemical residue evidence from the first millennium BCE in the Pamirs." *Science Advances* 5(6): eaaw1391 (12 June 2019). DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.aaw1391

**Finding:** Ten wooden braziers excavated from eight tombs at **Jirzankal Cemetery** (ca. 500–300 BCE) on the eastern Pamir Plateau, Xinjiang, China (altitude ~3,000 m / ~9,800 ft) yielded GC-MS-confirmed **cannabinol (CBN)** — the oxidative metabolite produced when THC combusts — from nine of the ten braziers and from charred stones inside them. The CBN signal was significantly stronger than that produced by low-THC wild cannabis, implying the mourners either selected high-cannabinoid plants or the Pamir altitude and UV exposure concentrated cannabinoid content. The ceremony involved heating stones inside wooden pan-shaped braziers and placing cannabis plants atop them — an inhalation ritual during mortuary rites.

**Location:** Jirzankal Cemetery, eastern Pamir Plateau, Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region, China.
**Method:** Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS) of organic residues from brazier interiors and stone surfaces.
**Significance:** Earliest directly dated, scientifically verified evidence of ritual cannabis **smoking** anywhere in the world.

**Fun fact:** The Jirzankal site sits at ~3,000 meters elevation. Researchers speculate that high-altitude UV radiation may naturally boost THC content in Pamir cannabis plants — the mountains themselves may have been a factor in selecting potent plants for ritual use.

**Artifact description:** The braziers are shallow, oval wooden bowls (roughly hand-sized) with scorched interiors. Inside lay small rounded stones bearing fire-blackened cannabis residue. Suitable illustration: a cutaway diagram of the brazier-and-stone assembly with cannabis flowers placed atop the heated stones, aromatic smoke rising in a mountain-tomb setting.

### 3. The Shaman's Stash: Yanghai Tomb Cannabis, ~700 BCE
**Citation (primary):** Russo E.B., Jiang H.E., Li X. et al. "Phytochemical and genetic analyses of ancient cannabis from Central Asia." *Journal of Experimental Botany* 59(15): 4171–4182 (2008). DOI: 10.1093/jxb/ern260. PMC2639026.

**Citation (follow-up):** Jiang H.E. et al. "Archaeobotanical studies of the Yanghai cemetery in Turpan, Xinjiang, China." *Archaeological and Anthropological Sciences* (2018). DOI: 10.1007/s12520-018-0719-2

**Finding:** Tomb M90 at the **Yanghai Tombs**, Turpan Basin, Xinjiang — grave of a male shaman (~45 years old at death) — contained **789 grams of cannabis** packed into a wooden bowl near his head and feet, preserved by the extreme aridity of the Gobi Desert. The cache dated to ~2,700 years ago. The cannabis consisted of female flowering tops (*sinsemilla*-type), with no hemp rope or fiber artifacts in the tomb, strongly indicating ritual/medicinal — not fiber — use. Microscopic botanical analysis, carbon dating, and genetic fingerprinting confirmed *Cannabis sativa*. The 2018 follow-up expanded the Yanghai archaeobotanical study to 25 plant species across the cemetery (ca. 1260–48 BCE), confirming cannabis' persistent shamanistic role.

**Location:** Yanghai Tombs, Turpan Basin, Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region, NW China.
**Method:** Microscopy, 14C AMS radiocarbon dating, genetic comparison (cpDNA), morphological analysis.
**Significance:** World's oldest confirmed cache of psychoactive cannabis, still green-tinged after 2,700 years of desert preservation.

**Fun fact:** The shaman's grave also contained a Chinese kongou (angular harp), archery equipment, and horse bridles — a toolkit for both this world and the next. The cannabis appears to have been his most important ritual possession.

**Artifact description:** A rough-hewn wooden bowl, brimming with dried female cannabis flower clusters, golden-brown from millennia in desert sands. Suitable illustration: a museum reconstruction showing the bowl beside the recumbent shaman, surrounded by his harp and bow.

### 4. Cannabis Burial Shroud, Jiayi Cemetery, ~2,500 Years Ago
**Citation:** Russo E.B., Dreher M., Mathre M.L. et al. (primary authors: Clarke R.C. & Merlin M.D.) "Ancient Cannabis Burial Shroud in a Central Eurasian Cemetery." *Economic Botany* 70(3): 213–221 (September 2016). DOI: 10.1007/s12231-016-9351-1

**Finding:** Thirteen **nearly whole *Cannabis sativa* plants** — radiometrically dated to 2,800–2,400 years ago — were found deliberately arranged over the body of a ~35-year-old man with Caucasian features at the **Jiayi Cemetery**, Turpan, NW China (Subeixi/Gushi culture). The plants were placed as a *burial shroud*, stem-to-root, flowering heads rich with glandular trichomes facing up. The flowering tops showed dense resin-producing glandular trichomes typical of high-THC drug-type cannabis. No fiber use was identified. This is the first time complete cannabis plants — not seeds or fragments — were recovered from an ancient burial.

**Location:** Jiayi Cemetery, Turpan, Xinjiang, China (240 graves excavated at site).
**Method:** Morphological analysis of preserved plant material; AMS radiocarbon dating; trichome microscopy.
**Significance:** First whole-plant cannabis archaeological find; first documented use of intact cannabis plants as a human burial good.

**Fun fact:** The Turpan Depression, where the Jiayi cemetery sits, is one of the hottest and driest places on Earth — a natural preservation chamber. Temperatures regularly exceed 45°C in summer, but the extreme aridity mummified the cannabis plants with extraordinary fidelity.

**Artifact description:** Thirteen cannabis plants, each over half a meter long, laid side-by-side over the deceased like a green blanket. The golden-yellow preserved flower clusters are studded with visible resin glands. Illustration idea: close-up drawing of a trichome-covered cannabis bract alongside a diagram of the burial layout.

### 5. Cannabis on Iron Age Israelite Altars, Tel Arad, ~700 BCE
**Citation:** Arie E., Rosen B. & Namdar D. "Cannabis and Frankincense at the Judahite Shrine of Arad." *Tel Aviv* 47(1): 5–28 (2020). DOI: 10.1080/03344355.2020.1732046

**Finding:** Two limestone altars from the "holy of holies" of the Iron Age shrine at **Tel Arad**, Beer-sheba Valley, Israel (in use ca. 760–715 BCE, during the reigns of Ahaz and Hezekiah) were subjected to organic residue analysis. The **smaller altar** yielded **THC, CBD, and CBN**, plus terpenes and terpenoids consistent with cannabis inflorescences burned in animal dung (the dung acting as the slow-burning fuel). The larger altar showed frankincense residue. The quantity of THC preserved was judged sufficient to induce psychoactive effects. This is the **earliest evidence of cannabis use in the Ancient Near East**.

**Location:** Tel Arad, Beer-sheba Valley, Negev, Israel. Shrine at the Israelite fortress (Iron Age IIA–B).
**Method:** GC-MS organic residue analysis of material scraped from altar surfaces; identification of cannabinoids and terpene profiles.
**Significance:** Provides the first chemical proof that cannabis was used in ancient Israelite religious ceremonies, likely to induce ecstatic states in worshippers entering the sanctuary.

**Fun fact:** The Tel Arad shrine is one of only two known Israelite temples outside Jerusalem. Its two altars — one for cannabis, one for frankincense — suggest a carefully choreographed ritual: incense to purify, cannabis to alter the mind.

**Artifact description:** Two square limestone altars, roughly 50 cm tall, their upper surfaces blackened by centuries of offerings. On the smaller altar, traces of cannabis resin embedded in organic ash survive after 2,700 years. Suitable illustration: the pair of altars in situ in the shrine's inner sanctum, with visible burn marks.

### 6. Bronze Age Cannabis Seeds at Haimenkou, Yunnan, ~1650–400 BCE
**Citation:** Dal Martello R., Min R., Stevens C.J., Qin L. & Fuller D.Q. "Morphometric approaches to *Cannabis* evolution and differentiation from archaeological sites: interpreting the archaeobotanical evidence from bronze age Haimenkou, Yunnan." *Vegetation History and Archaeobotany* 33: 503–520 (published online 30 November 2023). DOI: 10.1007/s00334-023-00966-6. PMC11127845.

**Finding:** Over **800 cannabis achenes (seeds)** were recovered from the 2008 excavation of **Haimenkou**, Yunnan, SW China — the largest prehistoric site in Yunnan (~5 hectares), characterized by pile dwellings on waterlogged ground, dating to 1650–400 BCE. Morphometric analysis (seed measurements) compared against a modern reference framework of wild, fiber, and drug-type cannabis revealed the Haimenkou seeds represent cannabis "in transition" — larger than wild types but not yet fully domesticated into fiber or drug specialization. The assemblage indicates early-stage domestication with beginnings of selection for larger oilseed/fiber traits.

**Location:** Haimenkou site, Yunnan Province, SW China.
**Method:** Morphometric seed measurement; comparative framework against modern *Cannabis* seed databases; waterlogged archaeobotanical flotation.
**Significance:** Demonstrates that cannabis domestication was an ongoing process across different Chinese regions simultaneously, not a single event — and that SW China had its own distinct early-cultivated lineage.

**Fun fact:** The Haimenkou site's seeds were preserved by **waterlogging** — the opposite preservation mechanism from the desert-dried Xinjiang finds. Two entirely different climates, both accidentally perfect cannabis museums.

### 7. Phytolith Evidence: Cannabis as a Core Crop, Shandong, 4,500–3,400 BP
**Citation:** [Lead authors from Shandong University and Chinese Academy of Sciences.] "Integrating cannabis into the prehistoric crop assemblage: Phytolith evidence from Shandong, Northern China (4500–3400 BP)." *Journal of Archaeological Science* (2026). DOI: 10.1016/j.jas.2026.000750 (ScienceDirect: S0305440326000750)

**Finding:** Phytolith analysis (microscopic silica plant-cell casts) of 132 samples from **Beitaishang** and **Qianzhongzitou** settlements in Shandong Province, northern China, dated to the Late Neolithic Longshan and Yueshi periods (4,500–3,400 BP), found cannabis phytoliths in **68.8% of Beitaishang samples** and **72.3% of Qianzhongzitou Longshan-period samples**. Cannabis phytoliths consistently co-occurred with millet and rice, and were concentrated in domestic features (ash pits, house floors), demonstrating that cannabis was **deeply integrated** into everyday agricultural life — not a marginal or ritual-only crop — thousands of years earlier than previously confirmed.

**Location:** Beitaishang and Qianzhongzitou sites, Shandong Province, northern China.
**Method:** Phytolith extraction, morphological identification, and statistical comparison; samples from domestic archaeological contexts.
**Significance:** Pushes the confirmed integration of cannabis as a systematic staple crop in northern Chinese farming communities back by millennia, reframing it alongside millet and rice as a foundational Neolithic crop.

**Fun fact:** Phytoliths are essentially plant ghosts — when a plant decays, its cells leave behind hollow silica casts. Cannabis phytoliths are morphologically distinctive enough to identify the genus even after 4,500 years in agricultural soil.

### 8. Resurrecting Ancient Cannabinoid Enzymes: When THC Evolved
**Citation:** Villard C., Baser I. et al. (Wageningen University & Research). "Resurrected Ancestral Cannabis Enzymes Unveil the Origin and Functional Evolution of Cannabinoid Synthases." *Plant Biotechnology Journal* (2025). DOI: 10.1111/pbi.70475. PubMed: 41454532.

**Finding:** Dutch researchers at Wageningen University used **ancestral sequence reconstruction** to computationally recreate and then synthesize cannabis enzymes as they existed **more than 25 million years ago** — before *Cannabis* and *Humulus* (hops) diverged into separate genera. These resurrected ancestral cannabinoid oxidocyclase enzymes were **promiscuous multi-taskers**, producing THCA, CBDA, and CBCA simultaneously. Over millions of years, gene duplication and natural selection refined them into today's highly specialized THCAS (producing only THC) and CBDAS (producing only CBD). A four-residue insertion in the enzyme's ASA-loop was the critical evolutionary step enabling product diversification. Crucially, the ancestral enzymes proved easier to produce in heterologous systems (bacteria, yeast), opening doors for biosynthetic cannabinoid production.

**Location:** Laboratory study (Wageningen University & Research, Netherlands).
**Method:** Ancestral sequence reconstruction; heterologous enzyme expression; functional biochemical assays; phylogenetic analysis.
**Significance:** Provides first experimental confirmation of *when* and *how* the THC/CBD biochemical pathway evolved, bridging paleobotany and biotechnology.

**Fun fact:** The ancestral cannabis enzyme that "invented" THC over 25 million years ago was a biochemical generalist — nature's chemist making a cocktail of cannabinoids before evolution settled on specific recipes. This ancient enzyme may be the key to producing novel medical cannabinoids in fermentation tanks.

## Methods Glossary

| Method | What It Does | Cannabis Application |
|--------|-------------|---------------------|
| **GC-MS** (Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry) | Separates and identifies volatile organic compounds by molecular mass | Detects cannabinoids (THC, CBD, CBN) in ancient residues on artifacts |
| **AMS Radiocarbon Dating** | Accelerator mass spectrometry measures 14C decay for precise dating | Dates cannabis plant material, seeds, and organic residues |
| **Whole-Genome Resequencing** | Sequences full genomes of many accessions; identifies SNPs | Reconstructs domestication history, geographic origins, selection events |
| **Morphometric Analysis** | Measures seed/fruit dimensions against reference databases | Distinguishes wild, fiber-type, and drug-type cannabis in archaeobotanical samples |
| **Phytolith Analysis** | Identifies silica micro-casts of plant cells preserved in soil | Detects cannabis presence in domestic contexts even when macroremains are absent |
| **Ancestral Sequence Reconstruction** | Computationally infers and synthesizes ancient enzyme sequences | Recreates pre-evolutionary cannabinoid biosynthesis pathways |

## Fun Facts

1. **The oldest confirmed cannabis stash** — 789 grams in a shaman's wooden bowl at Yanghai, Xinjiang — is still older than the oldest known written recipe and predates the founding of Rome by over 200 years.

2. **Cannabis and hops are cousins.** THCA synthase (the enzyme that makes THC) evolved from an ancestral enzyme shared with *Humulus lupulus* (hops, used in brewing). Your beer and your joint share a 25-million-year-old biochemical ancestor.

3. **High-altitude enhancement:** The Jirzankal braziers at 3,000 m elevation showed cannabis CBN signatures stronger than modern wild cannabis. Elevated UV radiation at altitude increases THC biosynthesis — the Pamir mourners may have been exploiting a natural geographic "upgrade."

4. **Phytoliths outlast everything.** At the Shandong sites, cannabis phytoliths survived 4,500 years in open domestic soil where no seeds, fibers, or leaves could have persisted — because silica doesn't rot, burn easily, or dissolve in most soils.

5. **Single origin, global spread.** All cannabis cultivars — from Oregon dispensary strains to Indian charas to European hemp — trace back genetically to a single domestication event in Neolithic China, spreading first along proto-Silk Road trade networks.

6. **The burial shroud cannabis had trichomes.** The 13 plants laid over the Jiayi Cemetery man still had intact resin-producing glandular trichomes visible under microscopy after 2,500 years in desert sand — the same structures that produce THC on modern plants.

7. **Two altars, two drugs.** At Tel Arad's "holy of holies," the deliberate pairing of a cannabis altar and a frankincense altar suggests a sophisticated, intentional psychopharmacological ritual — cannabis for psychoactivity, frankincense (which contains the anxiolytic compound incensole acetate) for calming — a Bronze Age guided meditation.

8. **Cannabis was boring (in the best way).** The Shandong phytolith data shows cannabis phytoliths in >68% of domestic samples alongside millet and rice — suggesting that for Neolithic farmers, cannabis was as mundane and essential as wheat is today.

## Summary

The last decade of archaeological science has fundamentally rewritten the deep history of cannabis. Genomic research (Ren et al., *Science Advances*, 2021) anchors domestication to East Asia ~12,000 years ago — a single origin from which all fiber and drug cultivars descend, with specialization emerging ~4,000 years ago. Chemical residue studies using GC-MS have located ritual smoking in the Pamir Mountains by 500 BCE (Ren et al., 2019) and cannabis burning on Israelite altars by 760 BCE (Arie et al., 2020) — the earliest Near Eastern evidence. Archaeobotanical fieldwork recovered the world's oldest psychoactive cache (789g in a Yanghai shaman's tomb, ~700 BCE; Russo et al., 2008), the first intact cannabis burial shroud (Jiayi Cemetery, ~2,500 BP; Clarke & Merlin, 2016), and over 800 seeds showing Bronze Age domestication-in-progress at Haimenkou, Yunnan (Dal Martello et al., 2023). Most recently, phytolith evidence from Shandong (2026) confirms cannabis was a core domestic crop alongside millet 4,500 years ago, while enzyme resurrection experiments (Villard et al., *Plant Biotechnology Journal*, 2025) trace THC biosynthesis itself back 25 million years to a common ancestor with hops. Taken together, these findings position cannabis not as a marginal intoxicant but as one of humanity's foundational cultivated plants — simultaneously a fiber source, food crop, medicine, shamanic tool, and ritual sacrament across three continents for at least 120 centuries.
