# Cannabis & Hashish in Asia: Research Dossier
*Region: China · India · Central Asia · Persia · Tibet · Nepal*
*Compiled: 2026-06-02 | Fetches used: ~18*

---

## Key Timeline Entries

### 1. c. 10,000 BCE — Hemp in Neolithic China
The earliest physical evidence of hemp use anywhere in the world comes from rope imprints on broken pottery shards excavated in China, dating to approximately 10,000 BCE. Hemp (*Cannabis sativa*, called *ma* 麻) was one of the first cultivated plants in China, exploited initially for fibre to make rope, cloth, and bowstrings. Seeds were also used as a grain crop. This represents the foundation of an unbroken tradition of hemp use in East Asia stretching across twelve millennia.

**Sources:** Russo (2007), *Cannabis and Cannabinoids* (Grotenhermen & Russo eds.); general archaeobotanical consensus.

---

### 2. c. 2700 BCE — Shennong Ben Cao Jing and the Divine Farmer
Chinese tradition credits the legendary Emperor Shennong (神農, "Divine Farmer") with first documenting the medical properties of cannabis in the *Shennong Ben Cao Jing* (神農本草經, *Divine Farmer's Classic of Materia Medica*). Although the text was only compiled in written form c. 100 CE from earlier oral and written sources, it is believed to encode knowledge from a much older tradition. The work classifies cannabis (*ma*) as a "superior" herb—a dual *yin/yang* plant—and lists its uses against malaria, rheumatism, constipation, beri-beri, and "female weakness."

**Extract:** "The fruit of hemp... if taken in excess will produce visions of devils. Over the long term, it makes one communicate with spirits and lightens one's body." *(Shennong Ben Cao Jing, reconstructed text; quoted in Russo 2007)*

**Fun fact:** The *Shennong Ben Cao Jing* lists 365 drugs — one for every day of the year — and cannabis is one of only a handful classified as a "superior" (non-toxic, life-prolonging) herb.

**Sources:** Li H-L (1974), *Economic Botany*; Russo E (2007); Touw M (1981), *Journal of Psychoactive Drugs*.

---

### 3. c. 700–500 BCE — Yanghai Shaman Burial, Turpan Basin
Excavations at the Yanghai Tombs near Turpan (Turfan), Xinjiang, China, uncovered the 2,700-year-old grave of a Caucasoid male interpreted as a shaman. Among his burial goods was a large cache of cannabis — approximately 789 grams of whole female plant tops — superbly preserved by the arid climate. Phytochemical and genetic analysis confirmed the presence of tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), cannabinol (CBN, an oxidative degradation product confirming original psychoactive potency), and the THC synthase enzyme. The cannabis was deliberately selected for its psychoactive properties and placed near the deceased's head and feet.

**Extract:** "The cannabis was presumably employed by this culture as a medicinal or psychoactive agent, or an aid to divination." *(Russo et al., Journal of Experimental Botany, 2008)*

**Sources:** Russo EB et al. (2008), "Phytochemical and genetic analyses of ancient cannabis from Central Asia," *Journal of Experimental Botany* 59(15): 4171–4182. DOI: 10.1093/jxb/ern260. Also: Jiang H-E et al. (2006), "A new insight into Cannabis sativa utilization from 2500-year-old Yanghai Tombs, Xinjiang, China," *Journal of Ethnopharmacology* 108(3): 414–422.

---

### 4. c. 500 BCE — Jirzankal Cemetery: Earliest Evidence of Intentional Cannabis Smoking
At the Jirzankal Cemetery on the Pamir Plateau in far-western China (Xinjiang/Tajikistan border), an international team identified chemical signatures of high-THC cannabis in ten wooden braziers (incense burners) excavated from eight tombs. Gas chromatography and mass spectrometry revealed unusually elevated THC levels—far above those in typical wild cannabis—confirming selection for psychoactive strains. The burials date by radiocarbon to c. 500 BCE. The enclosed burial chambers suggest mourners inhaled cannabis vapour as part of funerary rituals, representing the earliest direct archaeological evidence of intentional psychoactive cannabis inhalation.

**Extract (researcher quote):** "People may have been [burning cannabis] to communicate with the divine or the dead." — Dr. Robert Spengler III (archaeobotanist, Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology), quoted in *PBS Nova*, 2019.

**Sources:** Ren M et al. (2019), "The origins of cannabis smoking: Chemical residue evidence from the first millennium BCE in the Pamirs," *Science Advances* 5(6): eaaw1391. DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.aaw1391.

**Flag:** The Jirzankal site sits on the ancient precursor to the Silk Road; the high-THC cannabis may reflect deliberate cultivation and long-distance trade, not purely local wild plant use.

### 5. c. 500–400 BCE — Scythian Vapor Baths (Herodotus)
The Greek historian Herodotus, writing c. 440 BCE, described how Scythian nomads of the Eurasian steppe conducted cannabis vapor baths after funerals. Archaeological confirmation comes from Pazyryk-culture kurgans (burial mounds) in the Altai Mountains of Siberia, where excavations in 1947 revealed wooden-frame tents with bronze cauldrons containing hemp seeds and heated stones — exactly as Herodotus described. This places cannabis at the heart of Scythian ritual life across a vast territory from the Black Sea to western China.

**Extract (Herodotus, Rawlinson translation):** "The Scythians take some of this hemp-seed, and, creeping under the felt coverings, throw it upon the red-hot stones; immediately it smokes, and gives out such a vapour as no Grecian vapour-bath can exceed; the Scyths, delighted, shout for joy, and this vapour serves them instead of a water-bath." *(Herodotus, Histories, Book IV, ch. 75, c. 440 BCE)*

**Sources:** Herodotus, *Histories* IV.73–75 (Rawlinson trans.); Rudenko SI (1970), *Frozen Tombs of Siberia*; Charters et al. confirmed cannabinoids in Pazyryk brazier residue.

### 6. c. 1500–1000 BCE & medieval flowering — Bhang and the Atharva Veda, India
The Atharva Veda (compiled c. 1500–1000 BCE) is the oldest Sanskrit text to mention *bhanga* (cannabis), listing it as one of five sacred plants that "release us from anxiety." The verse in question (AV 11.6.15) pairs hemp with barley and darbha grass as protective gifts: *"To the five kingdoms of the plants which Soma rules as Lord we speak: darbha, hemp, barley, mighty power: may these deliver us from woe."* (Griffith translation.) Cannabis also appears in the Sushruta Samhita (c. 600 BCE) as a medicinal plant recommended for catarrh, phlegm, and diarrhoea. Scholastic caution: while the Vedic *bhanga* is identified with cannabis by most modern scholars, this identification was absent from Sanskrit commentary until the medieval period (c. 11th–13th cent. CE).

**Extract:** "We speak of the five plants...hemp, barley...may these deliver us from woe." *(Atharva Veda 11.6.15, tr. Ralph T. H. Griffith, 1895)*

**Sources:** Griffith RTH (1895), tr. *Hymns of the Atharva Veda*; Chopra & Chopra (1957), *Indian Journal of Medical Research*; Sharma et al. (2023), *Cannabis Research Review*, PMC10011848.

**Flag:** The identification of Vedic *bhanga* with psychoactive cannabis is widely accepted but not universally confirmed; some scholars note the intoxicating properties of bhang are not clearly described in Sanskrit religious texts until the medieval period.

### 7. c. 11th–13th century CE — Shiva, Sadhus, and Charas: Hindu Sacred Use
By the medieval period, cannabis was deeply embedded in Shaivite Hinduism. Shiva — destroyer and ascetic — became known as *Bhola Nath* ("Lord of Bhang") and is mythologically associated with using cannabis to sustain meditation. The Shiva Purana and later devotional texts describe him as wandering in the high Himalayas, sustained by cannabis. Sadhus (renunciant holy men) of Shaivite sects smoke cannabis (*ganja*) in clay pipes (*chillums*) as a sacramental act, emulating Shiva's asceticism. In Nepal and northern India, the festival of *Mahashivaratri* features open distribution of bhang. Charas — hand-rubbed hashish from live cannabis resin — was produced in the Himalayan foothills (Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, Nepal) and recognized by the 1700s as the world's finest resin concentrate.

**Extract:** "Bhang is the joy-giver, the sky-flier, the heavenly guide, the poor man's heaven, the soother of grief." *(Indian Hemp Drugs Commission Report, 1894, quoting a popular Marathi verse)*

**Sources:** Indian Hemp Drugs Commission Report (1894), 7 vols.; Sharma SK (2023), PMC10011848; Touw M (1981).

---

### 8. c. 13th century CE — Sufi Hashish in Persia: Sheikh Haydar and the Haydariyya Order
In Islamic Persia (Khorasan), the Sufi saint Qutb al-Din Haydar (d. 1221 CE) is credited — apocryphally — with "discovering" hashish. Egyptian historian al-Maqrizi (1364–1442) records the legend: after years of silent retreat, Haydar wandered into fields and tasted an unusual plant, returning to his disciples visibly transformed. He founded the Haydariyya dervish order, whose members consumed hashish publicly and used it in ritual self-mortification acts. Persian Sufi groups also consumed cannabis in a yoghurt drink called *dugh-e vahdat* ("drink of unity"). The al-Jawbari (13th c., Iraq) wrote that Haydariyya dervishes "took hashish before staging their performances of self-mutilation, in order to numb the pain." Pre-Islamic Zoroastrian texts compiled c. 559–379 BCE also mention *bhang* as a means to spiritual enlightenment, calling it *shahdaneh* ("royal seed").

**Extract (primary source):** "His shaykh, the Master Haydar, practiced much mystical exercise and exertion and used little food...he showed us the plant. He told us to take a leaf and eat it, which we did. Then, we returned to the monastery, finding in our hearts an irrepressible joy and gladness." *(al-Maqrizi, 14th–15th c., paraphrased in Laufer 1919 and Rosenthal 1971)*

**Sources:** al-Maqrizi (14th–15th c.); al-Jawbari (13th c.), *Kashf al-Asrar*; Rosenthal F (1971), *The Herb: Hashish versus Medieval Muslim Society*; Wikipedia: Cannabis in Iran (citing Laufer 1919).

**Flag:** Al-Maqrizi's account is legendary and disputed; hashish was certainly known in the Arab world before Haydar's time, as documented in 11th-century Egyptian sources.

---

### 9. c. 7th–12th century CE — Cannabis in Tibetan Tantric Buddhism
Ample textual evidence establishes cannabis use within certain strands of Vajrayana (Tantric) Buddhism practiced in the Tibeto-Himalayan region. The *Mahakala Tantra* (associated with the wrathful deity Mahakala) contains chapters incorporating cannabis in medicinal and ritual formulas. Forty-two of its fifty chapters include formulas for medicinal plants, many psychoactive, including *Cannabis sativa* in forms including leaves and resin. The Tibetan *Dictionary of Materia Medica* mentions cannabis as a *rasayana* (rejuvenating elixir). Mahayana tradition also holds that during the six years before his enlightenment, the Buddha subsisted on one hemp seed per day — a claim reflecting the plant's sacred status, though scholarly authenticity of this specific legend is debated.

**Sources:** Stablein WG (1976), "The Mahakala Tantra: A Theory of Ritual Blessings and Tantric Medicine," Columbia University dissertation; Parker & Lux, "Psychoactive Plants in Tantric Buddhism: Cannabis and Datura Use in Indo-Tibetan Esoteric Buddhism."

**Flag:** Mainstream Theravada and Tibetan institutional Buddhism generally prohibit intoxicants under the Fifth Precept; the Tantric use of cannabis was confined to specific esoteric lineages.

## Fun Facts

1. **The Yanghai shaman's stash weighed nearly 800 grams** — one of the largest single finds of ancient cannabis ever excavated, and forensic analysis confirmed it still contained detectable THC after 2,700 years in the desert sand. *(Russo et al. 2008)*

2. **Herodotus got it right.** He described Scythian hemp vapor baths in 440 BCE; archaeologists in 1947 excavated the Pazyryk kurgans in Siberia and found the exact tent frames and seed-filled bronze cauldrons he described — 2,400 years of accurate oral tradition confirmed by the spade. *(Rudenko 1970)*

3. **The word "canvas" derives from "cannabis."** The Old French *canevas* comes from Latin *cannabis*, because the tough hemp fibre was the dominant material for making cloth and sail canvas across Eurasia. *(OED)*

4. **Bhang is technically legal in India** even today, because the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act (1985) only banned cannabis resin (charas) and flowering tops — not the leaves used to make bhang — maintaining a loophole rooted in the 1894 Hemp Drugs Commission's conclusion that moderate bhang use caused "no appreciable harm." *(NDPS Act 1985; Indian Hemp Drugs Commission 1894)*

5. **The Jirzankal cemetery sits at over 3,000 metres altitude** in the Pamirs — researchers noted that higher altitude *Cannabis* plants often produce more THC as a UV-protection mechanism, which may partly explain why the Jirzankal strains showed elevated THC far above wild-type plants from lowland regions. *(Ren et al. 2019)*

6. **Sheikh Haydar supposedly swore his disciples to secrecy** about the location of the cannabis fields, vowing to share the secret with no one "but the destitute dervishes of God." The legend was considered historical fact for centuries before modern scholars like Franz Rosenthal placed it in its mythological context. *(Rosenthal 1971)*

7. **Nepalese Temple Balls** — spherical hand-rolled charas from the Himalayan foothills — were commercially exported via the Kathmandu Valley to India and beyond from at least the 18th century and were described by Francis Buchanan-Hamilton (1819) as the finest cannabis resin available anywhere. *(Buchanan-Hamilton 1819, cited in Sensi Seeds)*

8. **The Li Shizhen paradox:** China's greatest herbalist Li Shizhen (1518–1593) included cannabis in his *Bencao Gangmu* (1596) — covering 1,892 drugs in 52 volumes — but categorised its psychoactive effects under the heading of harmful excess, listing "seeing devils" as a symptom of overconsumption, while endorsing its seeds as a gentle laxative and nutritive. *(Bencao Gangmu, 1596)*
