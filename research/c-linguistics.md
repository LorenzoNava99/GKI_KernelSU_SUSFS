**Research Facet C — Word-Spread Dataset**
*Compiled 2026-06-02*

## 1. Core Terms with Scripts, Transliterations & Attestations

### Arabic

**حشيش** — *ḥashīsh*
- Literal meaning: "dried herb / fodder / cut grass" (from root *ḥ-sh-sh*, "to mow, to dry")
- Earliest attestation as slang for cannabis: epistle of Fatimid Caliph al-Āmir bi-Aḥkām Allāh, c. 1123 CE (used the derived form *ḥashīshiyya* as a pejorative for Syrian Nizaris); as a straightforward drug term in 13th-century Arabic medical and moralistic writing
- Source: Etymonline; New World Encyclopedia; Daftary, *The Assassin Legends* (1994)

**حشّاش / حشّاشين** — *hashshāsh / hashshāshīn*
- Literal meaning: "habitual consumer of hashish" / "hashish-eaters" (active-participle agent noun + plural suffix -īn)
- Note: The plural *hashshāshīn* was the Arabic pejorative applied to Nizari Ismailis, picked up by Crusaders and transmitted into European languages as "assassin." Daftary argues the term meant "low-class rabble" rather than literal drug-users.
- Source: Daftary, *The Assassin Legends: Myths of the Ismailis* (London: I.B. Tauris, 1994); IIS.ac.uk

### Persian / Urdu / Central Asian

**بنگ** — *bang* (Pers.) / *banj* (Arabic borrowing from Persian)
- Literal meaning: "intoxicating plant / hemp narcotic"
- Etymological note: Middle Persian *bang*; Book Pahlavi *mang*; borrowed into Arabic as *banj*. The Avestan root is debated; possibly cognate with Sanskrit *bhanga*.
- Earliest attestation: Middle Persian (Pahlavi) medical texts, c. 3rd–7th century CE; Arabicized *banj* appears in 9th-century Arabic pharmacopoeias (Ibn Sīnā, *al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb*)
- Source: Encyclopaedia Iranica, "BANG" entry

**چرس** — *charas*
- Literal meaning: Derived from Persian for "leather bag / wallet" (the bag in which pressed hemp resin was traded)
- Note: The word became a metonym — the container name transferred to the product. Possibly also connected to the leather pouches carried by Sufi dervishes who used cannabis.
- Earliest attestation: Medieval Persianate Central Asian trade contexts; enters Hindi/Urdu with Mughal-era cannabis culture (c. 16th century CE)
- Source: The Real Seed Company (landrace.blog); Encyclopaedia Iranica

### Sanskrit / Hindi

**भांग** — *bhāṅg* (Hindi) / *bhanga* (Sanskrit भङ्ग)
- Literal meaning: "breaking / that which breaks [the mind]" or possibly from the PIE root for hemp fiber
- Earliest attestation: Atharvaveda (c. 1500–1000 BCE) lists *bhanga* as one of five sacred anxiety-relieving plants; philological debate persists whether this Vedic *bhanga* = narcotic cannabis
- Source: Cannabis in India (Wikipedia); Wisdomlib; Bhang (Wikipedia)

**गांजा** — *gāñjā*
- Literal meaning: Uncertain; from Sanskrit *gañjā*, a powerful preparation from *Cannabis sativa*; possibly connected to the same PIE root (*kan/gan*) as cannabis
- Earliest attestation: Vedic Sanskrit roots, but the distinct drug-specific usage solidified in classical Sanskrit texts; borrowed into Portuguese, English, and Jamaican Creole (→ "ganja")
- Source: Ganja (Wikipedia); Wiktionary

**चरस** — *caras* (Hindi) — see Persian *charas* above

**विजया** — *vijayā*
- Literal meaning: "she who conquers / the victorious one"
- Note: Ayurvedic and tantric epithet for cannabis; also called *samvid* in some tantric texts. Reflects the plant's association with the goddess Durga / Devi.
- Earliest attestation: Ayurvedic literature, c. 1st millennium CE
- Source: THC Store India; Sangam Talks / Priya Mishra

### Hebrew (Disputed)

**קְנֵה-בֹשֶׂם** — *qāneh-bōśem*
- Literal meaning: *qāneh* = "reed / stalk"; *bōśem* = "spice / balsam" → "fragrant reed / aromatic stalk"
- Dispute: Polish anthropologist Sula Benet (1936) argued this term in Exodus 30:23 (the holy anointing oil recipe) refers to cannabis, pointing to a resemblance between Semitic *kanabos* and Scythian *cannabis*. Mainstream scholarship (e.g., Michael Zohary, *Plants of the Bible*, 1982) identifies *qāneh-bōśem* as *Acorus calamus* (sweet flag). Benet's Semitic-origin theory for the word *cannabis* itself was not widely accepted; linguists generally favour an Iranian/Scythian origin.
- Source: Cannabis Culture; Ancient Hebrew Research Center; Benet, "Tracing One Word through Different Languages" (1936)

### Greek

**κάνναβις** — *kánnabis*
- Literal meaning: No native Greek meaning; a direct loan-transcription of a Scythian or Thracian word
- Earliest attestation: Herodotus, *Histories* IV.74–75 (c. 440 BCE) — describes Scythians throwing hemp seeds on hot stones in vapor baths
- Note: The Scythian source word *kanab-* also gave Proto-Germanic *\*hanapiz* → Old English *hænep* → **hemp**, via Grimm's Law (PIE *k* → Germanic *h*, PIE *b* → Germanic *p*)
- Source: Etymology of cannabis (Wikipedia); Herodotus (Loeb edition)

**χασίς** — *chasís* (Modern Greek)
- A later borrowing from Arabic *ḥashīsh*, reflecting the 19th-century European diffusion of the term.

### Chinese

**麻** — *má*
- Literal meaning: The character depicts hemp stalks hung to dry (two stalks under a roof — 广 + two 木); it means "hemp / numbing" and by extension "cannabis"
- Earliest attestation: Oracle bone script and early bronze-age texts; *má* listed in the *Shennong Bencao Jing* (Divine Farmer's Classic of Materia Medica, compiled c. 100–200 CE, attributed traditions going back to c. 2700 BCE)
- Archaeological note: Cannabis fiber imprints found on Yangshao culture pottery near Xi'an, dated 4115–3535 BCE

**大麻** — *dàmá*
- Literal meaning: "great hemp / big hemp" — the modern scientific/colloquial term for *Cannabis sativa*
- Source: Frontiers in Pharmacology (2017); Shennong Bencao Jing; cannabis-in-China sources

### Turkish (Ottoman)

**esrar** — *esrār* (اسرار in Arabic script)
- Literal meaning: "secrets / mysteries" — Arabic *asrār*, plural of *sirr* ("secret")
- Note: An extraordinary semantic shift: Sufi poets used *esrār* ("secrets of the divine") as a code-word for cannabis, likely because of the drug's association with mystical insight in dervish circles. By the 16th century the cannabis sense was firmly established in Ottoman lyric poetry (*divan* literature), notably in the works of the dervish poet Ḳayġusuz Abdal.
- Source: Mavi Boncuk blog; Academia.edu, "Cannabis (esrār): A Unique Semantic Field in Classical Ottoman Lyric Poetry"

### Moroccan Arabic

**كيف** — *kīf* (also romanized *kef*)
- Literal meaning: From Arabic *kayf* — "well-being / pleasure / enjoyment / mood"
- Note: The term is native to North African Arabic (Maghrebi) and refers to cannabis flowers smoked, traditionally blended with tobacco, in a long-stemmed *sebsi* pipe. The French verb *kiffer* ("to like intensely") is borrowed from this term, a loanword into French slang via Algerian Arabic.
- Cannabis in Morocco: likely introduced by Arab traders c. 10th century CE; concentrated in the Rif mountains
- Source: Wiktionary; Riz Fez blog; PMC 9587784

## 2. The "Assassin" ↔ Hashishi Debate

The word **assassin** entered European languages through the Crusader encounter with the Nizari Ismaili sect based at Alamut (Iran) and Masyaf (Syria). The standard Western etymology traces it as:

> Arabic *hashshāsh* ("hashish-user") → plural *hashshāshīn* → Italian *assassino* → English *assassin*

This derivation was systematised by the French Orientalist **Silvestre de Sacy** in his 1809 *Mémoire* to the Institut National, where he also relied on **Marco Polo's** legendary account (c. 1298) of the "Old Man of the Mountain" drugging recruits with hashish in a paradise garden.

**Farhad Daftary's challenge** (*The Assassin Legends: Myths of the Ismailis*, I.B. Tauris, 1994) argues:

1. The **earliest datable use** of *hashīshī* (as a group label) is c. **1122–1123 CE**, in a Fatimid caliph al-Āmir's epistle attacking the Syrian Nizaris — used as a **social slur** meaning "rabble" or "people of low morals," not a literal drug accusation.
2. There is **no credible medieval evidence** that Nizari Ismailis systematically used hashish as part of their operations.
3. The word was a **pejorative term of abuse** that Crusaders and European travellers adopted phonetically without understanding its connotations.
4. Daftary notes the alternative Arabic term ***asāsiyyūn*** ("fundamentalists / those faithful to the foundation") may be the actual self-designation of the Nizaris, further muddying the etymology.
5. European ears dropped the Arabic plural suffix *-īn* and treated the resulting *hashīshī-* stem as the whole word, converting it into a noun meaning "murderer for hire."

**Conclusion**: The *hashishi* → *assassin* chain is the most phonologically plausible derivation, but the semantic link to drug use is almost certainly a **propaganda myth** — a pejorative insult that European sources took literally.

## 4. Timeline Entries

**Entry 1 — c. 440 BCE**
Herodotus transcribes the Scythian word *kanab-* as Greek *kánnabis* while describing Scythian hemp-steam baths. This is the earliest phonetic record of the cannabis word-root in a datable Western text, connecting Central Asian steppe nomads to the European linguistic tree via a single transliteration.
Themes: [ancient world, Greek, Scythian, ethnobotany]
Source: Herodotus, *Histories* IV.74–75 (c. 440 BCE)

**Entry 2 — c. 1123 CE**
Fatimid Caliph al-Āmir uses *hashīshiyya* as a political slur against Syrian Nizari Ismailis in an official epistle — the earliest dated appearance of the *hashīsh-* word family in a non-botanical context. The term means "rabble," not drug-users. Crusaders, mishearing and misunderstanding, begin transmitting the word westward.
Themes: [Arabic, medieval Islam, politics, etymology, assassin myth]
Source: Daftary, *The Assassin Legends* (1994); New World Encyclopedia

**Entry 3 — c. 1809 CE**
French Orientalist Silvestre de Sacy presents his *Mémoire* linking the Crusader term "assassin" to Arabic *hashshāshīn*, cementing the hashish-murder myth in European scholarship. He relies partly on Marco Polo's 13th-century traveler's tale. Daftary (1994) later systematically dismantles this as orientalist misreading of a social insult.
Themes: [European orientalism, Arabic, etymology, colonial scholarship]
Source: de Sacy, *Mémoire* (1809); Daftary (1994)

**Entry 4 — c. 15th–16th century CE**
Ottoman dervish poet Ḳayġusuz Abdal introduces *esrār* ("secrets/mysteries") as a literary code-word for cannabis in Ottoman lyric poetry. The semantic borrowing transforms a Sufi theological concept into a drug term, encoding the mystical dimension of intoxication within the very name of the substance.
Themes: [Ottoman, Turkish, Sufism, poetry, semantics]
Source: Academia.edu, "Cannabis (esrār): A Unique Semantic Field in Classical Ottoman Lyric Poetry"

**Entry 5 — 1936 / 1982 CE**
Polish anthropologist Sula Benet publishes her argument (elaborated 1975) that Hebrew *qāneh-bōśem* in Exodus 30:23 is cannabis, citing phonetic similarity to Semitic *kanabos*. Botanist Michael Zohary (*Plants of the Bible*, 1982) disputes this, identifying the plant as *Acorus calamus*. The debate remains unresolved and is a recurring flashpoint in cannabis history discourse.
Themes: [Hebrew, Biblical, botanical identification, linguistics dispute]
Source: Benet (1936, 1975); Zohary, *Plants of the Bible* (1982); Cannabis Culture

## 5. Linguistic Fun Facts

1. **Hemp and cannabis are the same word, twice.** Old English *hænep* (→ "hemp") and Greek *kánnabis* (→ "cannabis") both descend from the same Scythian root *kanab-*, but via different routes: hemp traveled through Proto-Germanic with Grimm's Law shifting *k→h* and *b→p*, while cannabis came via Greek and Latin with no consonant shift. English therefore has two words for the same plant from the same ancient source word.

2. **"Secrets" became slang.** Turkish *esrār* is the Arabic plural of *sirr* ("secret"), a word central to Islamic mystical theology. Sufi dervishes used it as a coded reference to cannabis precisely because the drug was associated with mystical hidden knowledge — then the code became the common word, and today most Turkish speakers have no idea "secrets" once meant "weed."

3. **The assassins may have been named for their social class, not their drug habits.** Farhad Daftary's research shows that *hashīshī* was first a contemptuous Arabic term for the urban poor — people who could only afford *hashīsh* (cheap dried scraps) rather than proper food. Applied to Nizari Ismailis as a political insult in 1122 CE, it had nothing to do with drug-fuelled murder. Marco Polo's paradise-garden story, written 175 years later, is now considered literary embellishment.

4. **"Ganja" went global from Sanskrit via Jamaica.** The Sanskrit/Hindi *gāñjā* traveled to the Caribbean with South Asian indentured laborers after 1838, was adopted into Jamaican Creole, then entered global English via reggae culture in the 20th century — one of the longest documented single-word journeys in cannabis lexicography.

5. **The character 麻 is a picture of drying hemp.** The Chinese character *má* (hemp/cannabis) is a pictograph: the component 广 represents a roof or shed, and the two 木 elements inside represent stalks of hemp hanging to dry. The word simultaneously means "hemp," "numb/tingly" (as in 麻木, *mámù*), and "sesame" (in 芝麻, *zhīma*) — a testament to how central hemp fiber was in early Chinese civilization.

6. **Morocco's kif gave French teenagers a verb.** Arabic *kayf* ("pleasure, well-being") → Moroccan *kīf* (cannabis) → French slang *kiffer* ("to really like / to be into something") — a semantic journey from classical Arabic philosophy of well-being to the mouths of Parisian teenagers, with cannabis culture as the bridge.

7. **Sanskrit had two separate words for hemp and cannabis.** Linguists note that Proto-Indo-Iranian distinguished between *śaṇa-* (industrial fiber hemp, ancestor of Sanskrit *śaṇa*) and *bhanga* (narcotic hemp). The Scythians, who bridged Central Asia, maintained the same distinction. This suggests that humans recognized and named the drug/fiber duality of *Cannabis sativa* at least 3,000 years ago.

## Summary

The vocabulary of cannabis and hashish constitutes one of the richest etymological networks in world history, tracing human movement, trade, and cultural exchange from Central Asian steppes to Moroccan mountain valleys. The root *kanab-* — possibly Proto-Indo-European, certainly Scythian — spawned two parallel streams that flowed west: one through Greek *kánnabis* into Latin and European languages, the other through Proto-Germanic Grimm's Law into *hemp*. Separately, Indo-Iranian languages developed *bhanga/bang* for the narcotic variety, a term that moved south into Sanskrit, east into China as a loanword, and west into Arabic as *banj*. Arabic contributed its own innovation: *ḥashīsh* ("dried herb"), which by the 12th century had accreted a pejorative social meaning whose misreading by Crusaders created the English word "assassin" — one of the most dramatic etymological hoaxes in linguistic history. Ottoman Turkish layered Sufi mysticism onto the pharmacopoeia by repurposing *asrār* ("divine secrets") as a drug term. Moroccan Arabic used *kayf* ("pleasure") to name the local cannabis preparation, which eventually lent French teenagers a verb. Hebrew's *qāneh-bōśem* remains a live academic dispute. Taken together, these terms form a map not only of a plant's spread but of the civilizations, trade routes, and power structures through which it traveled.
