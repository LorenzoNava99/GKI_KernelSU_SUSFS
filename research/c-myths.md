# Myths, Legends & Literary Extracts — Hashish History Research

*Facet C | Compiled 2026-06-02 | Sources verified via web research*

## 1. Core Myths & Legends

### 1.1 The Assassins / Hashishin Myth

**The Myth:** The Nizari Ismaili warrior-sect known as the Assassins (active c. 1090–1256 CE, headquartered at Alamut Castle in the Alborz Mountains of Persia) were so named because their leader drugged recruits with hashish. According to the standard legend — popularized by Marco Polo — the "Old Man of the Mountain" (Hassan-i Sabbah or his successors) would administer a soporific potion, transport young men into an artificial Paradise (a garden stocked with wine, food, and beautiful women), then reveal that this was the reward awaiting faithful assassins who died on missions. The word *assassin* itself was supposedly derived from Arabic *hashshashin* ("hashish-eaters").

**Marco Polo's Account (c. 1298):**
> "In a beautiful valley enclosed between two lofty mountains, he had built a luxurious garden, stored with every delicious fruit and fragrant shrub that could be procured… By means of small conduits in these buildings, streams of wine, milk, honey, and some of pure water, were seen to flow in every direction."
— *The Travels of Marco Polo*, Book I, Chapter XXIII (Yule translation)

> "So when the Old Man would have any Prince slain, he would say to such a youth: 'Go thou and slay So and So; and when thou returnest my Angels shall bear thee into Paradise.'"
— *Ibid.*

**The Debunking (Farhad Daftary):** Farhad Daftary, the leading modern scholar of Ismaili history (Institute of Ismaili Studies, London), demonstrated in *The Assassin Legends: Myths of the Isma'ilis* (1994) that the hashish story is a fabrication rooted in Crusader-era and hostile Sunni propaganda. Key points:
- Marco Polo wrote his account ~150 years after Hassan-i Sabbah's death (d. 1124) and had no direct knowledge; his sources were enemies of the Ismailis.
- No Ismaili source ever mentions hashish use as a recruitment tool.
- The term *hashshashin* was used by rival Muslims as a pejorative meaning "rabble" or "people of lax morals" — not necessarily "hashish-eaters." The exact etymology remains debated.
- The Nizari Ismailis were a sophisticated theological and political movement; their fighters were motivated by religious conviction, not pharmacological coercion.
- Modern scholarship (Bernard Lewis, Marshall Hodgson, Daftary) concurs: no credible historical evidence supports the hashish-paradise story.

**Fun Fact:** The English word *assassin* entered European languages via Italian (*assassino*) and Old French (*assassin*) from the Arabic, first appearing in Crusader chronicles around the 12th century — making it one of the very few Arabic loanwords in English with a cannabis-adjacent etymology, however disputed.

### 1.2 The Sheikh Haydar Legend

**The Myth:** The Persian Sufi master Qutb al-Din Haydar (d. 1221 CE), founder of the Haydariyya dervish order, personally "discovered" hashish after wandering alone into a field of hemp plants on a hot day. Unlike other plants that hung listlessly in the heat, the hemp stood erect and animated. Haydar ate the leaves, felt extraordinary joy, and returned to his disciples a transformed man. He shared the discovery with them and reportedly consumed cannabis until his death, ordering hemp planted around his grave.

**Rosenthal's Scholarship:** Franz Rosenthal's *The Herb: Hashish versus Medieval Muslim Society* (Brill, 1971) — the most comprehensive study of cannabis in medieval Islamic sources — notes that while Haydar's encounter is recorded in several Arabic chronicles (including that of Ibn al-Baitar, 13th c.), cannabis was demonstrably in use in the Islamic world before Haydar's time. The legend likely served to give Sufi hashish use a pious, divinely guided origin rather than record actual history. Rosenthal identifies the legend as a *foundation myth* for the spread of hashish among dervish orders from Persia into Egypt and the Levant after ~1200 CE.

### 1.3 Scythian Cannabis Rites (Herodotus)

**The Account:** The Greek historian Herodotus (c. 484–425 BCE) described the funerary purification rites of the Scythians — nomadic Iranian peoples of the Pontic steppe — in his *Histories*, Book IV, §§73–75:

> "The Scythians then take the seed of this hemp and, creeping under the felt coverings, throw it upon the red-hot stones; and, being so thrown, it smoulders and sends forth so much steam that no Greek vapour-bath could surpass it. The Scythians howl in their joy at the vapour-bath."
— Herodotus, *Histories* IV.75 (Godley translation, Loeb Classical Library)

Earlier (§73), Herodotus describes the preparation: "They make a booth by fixing in the ground three sticks inclined towards one another, and stretching around them woollen felts which they arrange so as to fit as close as possible: inside the booth a dish is placed upon the ground, into which they put a number of red-hot stones, and then add some hemp-seed."

**Archaeological Confirmation:** Excavations at the Pazyryk burial mounds (Altai Mountains, Siberia; 5th–3rd century BCE) in 1929 and 1947 discovered wooden frame tent-structures with copper cauldrons containing cannabis seed residue — a remarkable confirmation of Herodotus's account. Further confirmation came from Scythian kurgan finds near Stavropol, Russia, where gold vessels tested positive for both cannabis and opium residues, consistent with ritual use.

**Status:** One of the best-documented ancient cannabis ritual accounts — corroborated by archaeology.

### 1.4 Shiva, Bhang, and Hindu Sacred Tradition

**The Legend:** According to Hindu tradition, cannabis (bhang) is intimately connected with Lord Shiva, the god of destruction and transformation. The principal mythological account comes from the *Samudra Manthan* (Churning of the Ocean): when the gods churned the cosmic ocean to produce the nectar of immortality, the lethal poison *Halahala* emerged first, threatening all creation. Shiva drank it to save the world, and bhang was used to cool and soothe his burning throat. In another version, Shiva retreated alone under the shade of a cannabis plant after a dispute with his family and, upon tasting its leaves, was refreshed and renewed — declaring it his favourite plant.

**Textual Roots:** Cannabis appears in the *Atharvaveda* (c. 1200–1000 BCE) as one of five sacred plants (*pancha-upalabdha*), described as a "liberator from anxiety." Later texts (c. 1000 BCE) record ritual bhang preparation by Shiva devotees. Bhang consumption at festivals — particularly *Maha Shivratri* and *Holi* — remains a living tradition across India, where licensed bhang shops operate legally in several states.

**Fun Fact:** The ritual bhang drink (*bhang thandai* — cannabis leaves ground with milk, spices, almonds, and rose water) is not considered a "drug" in classical Hindu jurisprudence but a sacred offering (*prasad*) to Shiva.

### 1.5 The Old Man of the Mountain — Perception vs. Reality

**The Myth:** "The Old Man of the Mountain" (*Shaykh al-Jabal*) was a figure of terror in medieval European imagination: an ageless, all-powerful puppetmaster who controlled his followers absolutely through the promise of paradise and the use of hashish. The title was applied by Crusaders first to Rashid al-Din Sinan (d. 1193), the Syrian head of the Nizari Ismailis, then retroactively to all Nizari leaders.

**The Reality:** Rashid al-Din Sinan was a sophisticated theological leader and statesman who corresponded with Saladin and Richard I of England. The "Old Man" terror-myth was partly constructed by Crusader chroniclers (including William of Tyre and Arnold of Lübeck) who were enemies of the Ismailis and had strong incentives to portray them as irrational fanatics. The myth also served as useful propaganda among rival Muslim powers. Daftary notes the legend accumulated literary layers over decades, with each retelling adding sensationalism.

### 1.6 The "Hashshashin" Etymology Myth

**The Myth:** That *assassin* derives cleanly from Arabic *hashshashin* meaning "hashish-users," implying the sect's killings were performed in a drug-crazed state.

**The Fact:** The etymology is genuinely disputed among philologists and historians. The Arabic root *hashsh* (meaning grass/herb) gives *hashish*, and *hashshash* means a habitual user. However, medieval Arabic texts also used *hashishiyya* as a general term of social contempt — "riff-raff," "low-class rabble" — without any necessary connection to cannabis use. Bernard Lewis (*The Assassins*, 1967) noted both meanings were in play, and the pejorative social meaning may have been primary. Daftary concludes the term was applied to Nizaris by their enemies as an insult comparable to calling someone a "drug addict" — it says more about the accusers' rhetoric than about actual practices.

---

### 1.7 Herodotus and the "Purification" Framing

**The Myth:** That Herodotus described the Scythian hemp rite purely as a funeral purification — a solemn, non-intoxicating ceremony.

**The Fact:** The Greek word Herodotus uses — *katharsis* (purification) — was indeed the stated function, but his description of the Scythians "howling with joy" (*ololyzousin*) makes plain the ritual also produced powerful intoxication. Modern ethnobotanists interpret this as a combined grief-purification and communal intoxication ritual following burial — the "howl" being both mourning and ecstasy. The ritual is now understood as one of the earliest documented deliberate cannabis inhalation practices, predating most written records by centuries.

## 2. Primary Literary Extracts

### Extract 1 — Herodotus, *Histories* IV.75 (c. 430 BCE)
> "The Scythians take the seed of this hemp and, creeping under the felt coverings, throw it upon the red-hot stones; and, being so thrown, it smoulders and sends forth so much steam that no Greek vapour-bath could surpass it. The Scythians howl in their joy at the vapour-bath."

**Attribution:** Herodotus, *Histories* Book IV, §75 (trans. A.D. Godley, Loeb Classical Library, 1920). c. 430 BCE.

---

### Extract 2 — Marco Polo, *The Travels* (c. 1298)
> "In a beautiful valley enclosed between two lofty mountains he had built a luxurious garden, stored with every delicious fruit and fragrant shrub that could be procured. Palaces of various sizes and forms were erected in different parts of the grounds, ornamented with works in gold, with paintings, and with furnishings of rich silks."

**Attribution:** Marco Polo, *Il Milione* (*The Travels of Marco Polo*), c. 1298; trans. H. Yule & H. Cordier, *The Book of Ser Marco Polo* (1903), Book I, Ch. XXIII ("Of the Old Man of the Mountain").

---

### Extract 3 — *Tale of the Hashish Eater* from *One Thousand and One Nights* (medieval; Burton trans. 1885)
> "He ate of the Banj [hashish-electuary] until he lost his senses and thereupon it seemed to him that the bath walls had fallen down and the roof had flown off... and he stood a Sultan in his palace, with the grandees of the realm before him."

**Attribution:** "The Tale of the Hashish-Eater," *Alf Layla wa-Layla* (*One Thousand and One Nights*); trans. Richard F. Burton, *The Book of the Thousand Nights and a Night*, Vol. X (1885). Medieval Arabic composition; precise date unknown.

---

### Extract 4 — W.B. O'Shaughnessy, "On the Preparations of Indian Hemp, or Gunjah" (1839)
> "The hemp plant is used as an intoxicating agent in a great variety of forms, by the Hindoos of all classes, from the monarch to the peasant... A tincture of the hemp resin I hold to be a most valuable addition to our list of genuine antispasmodics."

**Attribution:** W.B. O'Shaughnessy, "On the Preparations of the Indian Hemp, or Gunjah (*Cannabis indica*)," *Transactions of the Medical and Physical Society of Bengal*, 1838–40 (pub. 1839), pp. 421–461.

> "The patient was soothed, slept, and on the following day was free from pain. The antispasmodic powers of the remedy were thus decisively evinced."
**Attribution:** O'Shaughnessy, *ibid.* (describing cannabis treatment of a tetanus case).

---

### Extract 5 — Théophile Gautier, "Le Club des Hachichins" (*Revue des Deux Mondes*, 1846)
> "My body seemed to dissolve, and I became transparent. Within my breast I perceived the hashish I had eaten in the form of an emerald scintillating with a million points of fire... I was my own spirit and my body both at once."

**Attribution:** Théophile Gautier, "Le Club des Hachichins," *Revue des Deux Mondes*, February 1, 1846. (Trans. from the French.)

---

### Extract 6 — Jacques-Joseph Moreau de Tours, *Du hachisch et de l'aliénation mentale* (1845)
> "The hashish intoxication offers to philosophical study a series of phenomena of the greatest interest; it gives us the means of probing what happens in the mind of the mad — for madmen and hashish-eaters express themselves similarly when seeking to convey what they have experienced."

**Attribution:** J. Moreau de Tours, *Du hachisch et de l'aliénation mentale: études psychologiques* (Paris: Fortin, Masson, 1845). (Paraphrase/trans. from the French original: "Aliénés et mangeurs de hachisch s'expriment de même quand ils veulent faire comprendre ce qu'ils ont éprouvé.")

---

### Extract 7 — Charles Baudelaire, *Les Paradis Artificiels* (1860)
> "Wine exalts the will; hashish annihilates it. Wine is a physical support; hashish is the weapon of suicide... The man who takes resort to a poison in order to think will soon be unable to think without the poison."

**Attribution:** Charles Baudelaire, *Les Paradis Artificiels* (Paris: Poulet-Malassis et de Broise, 1860), "Le Poème du haschisch," Pt. V. (Trans.)

> "After a lapse of time, whose measure I cannot account for — seconds? minutes? hours? — a fresh sharpening of the senses announced itself. I seemed to hear the colours and to smell the sounds — phenomena which poets of all ages have attributed to an excited state of the soul."
**Attribution:** Baudelaire, *ibid.*, Pt. III.

---

### Extract 8 — Fitz Hugh Ludlow, *The Hasheesh Eater* (1857)
> "My head expanded wider and wider, revolving with inconceivable rapidity, and enlarging in space with every revolution. It filled the room — the house — the city; it became a world, peopled with the shapes of men and monsters."

**Attribution:** Fitz Hugh Ludlow, *The Hasheesh Eater: Being Passages from the Life of a Pythagorean* (New York: Harper & Brothers, 1857), Ch. I.

> "The years flew on; I heard the musical rush of their wings in the abyss outside of me, and from cycle to cycle, from life to life I careered, a mote in eternity and space."
**Attribution:** Ludlow, *ibid.*

---

### Extract 9 — Atharvaveda (c. 1200–1000 BCE) on bhang
> "We speak to the five kingdoms of plants with their lord Soma at their head: may it be propitious to thee, and may it deliver us from grief. Bhang is one of the five sacred plants."

**Attribution:** *Atharvaveda* XI.6.15 (approximate; trans. various). c. 1200–1000 BCE. (Note: the precise rendering of cannabis in this hymn is debated among Vedic scholars; the attribution of bhang to the *Atharvaveda* is widely cited in ethnobotanical literature.)

## 3. Timeline Entries

### Entry A — c. 430 BCE: Herodotus Documents Scythian Hemp Rites
The Greek historian Herodotus recorded in Book IV of his *Histories* the Scythian practice of inhaling cannabis vapour in felt-covered tents following burials, producing intense intoxication marked by ecstatic howling. This is the earliest unambiguous written record of deliberate cannabis inhalation for its effects. Archaeological finds at Pazyryk (1929, 1947) subsequently confirmed the practice with physical evidence of hemp-seed burners in burial mounds.
- **Themes:** ancient rites, archaeology, primary sources
- **Source:** Herodotus, *Histories* IV.73–75; Pazyryk excavations (Rudenko, 1953)

### Entry B — c. 1090–1256 CE: The Assassin Myth Takes Shape
From Hassan-i Sabbah's seizure of Alamut Castle (1090) through the Mongol conquest of the Nizari state (1256), European Crusaders and hostile Muslim rivals constructed the legend of the "Hashish-eating Assassins" — a story of pharmacological mind-control and a secret garden of paradise. Marco Polo's retelling (c. 1298) cemented the myth in Western imagination for seven centuries, until modern scholarship — especially Farhad Daftary's *The Assassin Legends* (1994) — exposed it as anti-Ismaili propaganda with no credible primary-source support.
- **Themes:** myth-busting, political propaganda, Islamic history, etymology
- **Source:** Daftary, *The Assassin Legends* (1994); Lewis, *The Assassins* (1967)

### Entry C — 1844–1849: Club des Hashischins, Paris
At the Hôtel Pimodan (now Hôtel de Lauzun) on the Île Saint-Louis, Dr. Jacques-Joseph Moreau de Tours hosted monthly séances at which Paris's literary elite — Baudelaire, Gautier, Nerval, Dumas, Delacroix — consumed *dawamesk* (a preparation of hashish blended with cinnamon, cloves, nutmeg, pistachio, butter, and sugar) and recorded their experiences. The club became a seedbed of both Romantic literature and early psychiatric research, with Moreau publishing his findings as *Du hachisch et de l'aliénation mentale* (1845).
- **Themes:** literary history, 19th-century France, psychopharmacology, Romanticism
- **Source:** Moreau de Tours (1845); Gautier, *Revue des Deux Mondes* (1846)

### Entry D — 1857: Fitz Hugh Ludlow, *The Hasheesh Eater*
American writer Fitz Hugh Ludlow (1836–1870), a student at Union College in Schenectady, New York, began experimenting with Tilden's cannabis extract obtained from a local pharmacy and kept detailed notes of his experiences over several years. Published in 1857, *The Hasheesh Eater* was the first full-length American drug memoir — describing temporal distortion, spatial expansion of consciousness, and terrifying "descent" phases — and created a popular craze for hashish candy and private hashish clubs in the United States.
- **Themes:** American literary history, drug memoir, 19th-century pharmacology
- **Source:** Ludlow (1857); Public Domain Review

### Entry E — 1860: Baudelaire, *Les Paradis Artificiels*
Charles Baudelaire (1821–1867) drew on his own experiences at the Club des Hashischins and the work of Thomas De Quincey to write *Les Paradis Artificiels* — part hashish phenomenology, part moral warning. Unlike most drug enthusiasts of his circle, Baudelaire ultimately condemned hashish as a "weapon of suicide" that destroys the will while giving the illusion of spiritual transcendence. The book remains a landmark of both drug literature and French prose-poetry.
- **Themes:** French Romanticism, literary history, moral philosophy, drug culture
- **Source:** Baudelaire (1860); Wikipedia, *Les Paradis artificiels*

## 4. Fun Facts

1. **The Word "Assassin" in 17 Languages:** Because *hashshashin* entered European languages through Crusader-era contact, the word *assassin* (or close cognates) appears in English, French, Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, German, Dutch, Russian, Polish, Turkish, and many other languages — making it one of cannabis's most globally distributed linguistic legacies.

2. **Herodotus Was Right:** For centuries, scholars debated whether Herodotus's account of Scythian hemp rites was a traveller's tall tale. The 1947 Pazyryk excavations settled it: actual bronze cauldrons with hemp-seed residue were found exactly as Herodotus described.

3. **Dawamesk Recipe:** The Club des Hashischins' hashish preparation (*dawamesk*, from the Arabic *da'amask*) typically combined cannabis resin with cinnamon, cloves, nutmeg, pistachio, sugar or honey, orange juice, butter — and sometimes *cantharides* (Spanish fly, a supposed aphrodisiac). The doses were served on Oriental porcelain dishes.

4. **Ludlow's Pharmacy:** Fitz Hugh Ludlow obtained his cannabis from a local apothecary — specifically "Tilden's Extract of Cannabis Indica," a commercially available tincture produced by Tilden & Co. of New Lebanaon, NY. Over-the-counter cannabis tinctures were common in 19th-century American pharmacies.

5. **Bhang Is Legal:** Bhang (cannabis leaves and seeds dissolved in liquid) remains legal to sell and consume in several Indian states — including Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, and Bihar — where licensed government bhang shops operate openly, particularly during Holi and Maha Shivratri.

6. **Marco Polo Never Visited Alamut:** Marco Polo's account of the Assassins and their garden paradise was almost certainly second-hand — he was likely in China during the period he describes the Alamut court, and Alamut itself was destroyed by the Mongols in 1256, decades before his journey (c. 1271–95).

7. **Moreau as Psychedelic Pioneer:** Moreau de Tours is considered a forerunner of modern psychopharmacology; his 1845 book proposed that drug-induced states could serve as a "window" into psychosis — an idea that anticipated 20th-century research with mescaline (Havelock Ellis, 1897) and LSD (Hofmann, 1943).

8. **The Haydar Grave:** According to legend, Sheikh Haydar specifically requested that his grave be planted with hemp so that its shade would fall on his followers — one of the earliest recorded examples of cannabis as a quasi-sacred memorial plant.
